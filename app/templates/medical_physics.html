What is medical physics?

Medical physics is the use of physics, computation, measurement, and radiation science to solve problems in medicine and biology. It can lead to clinical hospital work, imaging and AI, radiation protection, biophysics research, medtech, graduate school, and adjacent healthcare technology roles.

You are learning how to make invisible things measurable: radiation dose, image quality, scanner performance, biological structure, treatment uncertainty, and model reliability.

It is applied physics in a medical or biological setting.

The clinical version of medical physics is a regulated, graduate-level profession. The broader undergraduate ecosystem is bigger: imaging, radiation, biophysics, instrumentation, data science, health physics, and research all sit near the same toolkit.

Measurement

Calibrating machines, measuring dose, testing detector response, estimating uncertainty, and proving that systems perform as expected.

Modelling

Using equations, simulations, reconstruction algorithms, and statistical models to connect physical signals to biological or clinical decisions.

Translation

Turning physics into something useful for physicians, therapists, technologists, patients, researchers, regulators, or product teams.

Important distinctions

  • A medical physics undergraduate degree does not make you an independent clinical medical physicist by itself.
  • Clinical medical physics normally continues through CAMPEP-accredited graduate training, clinical residency-style training, and certification.
  • Radiation therapist, radiographer, sonographer, dosimetrist, biomedical engineer, and medical physicist are different roles.
  • The degree can still be useful even if you do not become a clinical physicist, because the technical toolkit transfers well.

Career prospects

What can you do with the degree?

The honest answer is: the degree opens doors when it is paired with evidence. Courses alone are rarely the whole story. Research, co-op, coding, clinical exposure, strong references, and a coherent track turn “medical and biological physics” into a convincing next-step profile.

Right after undergrad

Look for research assistant, lab technician, imaging/data analyst, QA, radiation safety assistant, clinical research, device support, scientific software, and graduate-school bridge roles.

After a master's or PhD

More doors open in medical physics residency competition, imaging science, biophysics research, R&D, algorithm development, industry scientist roles, and technical leadership tracks.

With clinical training

Residency and certification move you toward independent clinical medical physicist work in radiation oncology, diagnostic imaging, nuclear medicine, MRI, or related specialties.

Finding fit

Use the degree to test your direction before you commit.

A good path is not just the one with the best title. It should match the kind of problems you want to spend years solving.

If you like clinical responsibility Shadow, volunteer, talk to physicists, learn the CAMPEP and residency path, and prove you can handle careful QA-style work.
If you like code and images Build image-processing notebooks, learn validation, version your projects, and look for imaging, MRI, CT, PET, or AI research.
If you like biology and mechanisms Prioritize biophysics, microscopy, soft matter, molecular systems, and supervisors whose questions feel physically interesting.
If you like safety and systems Explore health physics, radiation protection, nuclear safety, technical regulation, field monitoring, and risk communication.
Bottom line: Medical physics is not only one job. Treat the degree as a platform: physics depth plus medical context plus evidence of what you can build, measure, analyze, or explain. Use the roadmap
